Sometimes, what seems like a complicated truth, can be summarised in just one line. Which is what the Indian Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman recently did, when she said: “Because of the ease of liquidity, ease of availability, people are even exiting easily.”
She was explaining why foreign institutional investors have been exiting Indian stocks in droves.
The FIIs have net sold stocks worth ₹4.9 trillion or $53 billion since April 2024 – the start of financial year 2024-25. In calendar year, 2026, they have net sold stocks worth ₹3.1 trillion or $33 billion.
Now, for sellers to be able to sell, the buyers need to be in the mood to buy. If the buyers are not in the mood, the sellers need to offer a lower price to incentivise the buyers. 
If the buyers are still not in the mood, the sellers need to offer an even lower price. 
This is how a market works until the buyers start buying or the sellers decide to hold back from selling.
The point being if the sellers decide to sell when buyers don’t want to buy, the prices will fall. In case of the stock market, if the sellers are desperate to sell, the stock market may even crash. 
Now, the sellers, that is the FIIs in this case, understand this. And unless they are really desperate to exit, they won’t crash the Indian stock market.
In the last few years, as the FIIs have sold, the stock market has fallen a little, but it hasn’t crashed.
The FIIs have mostly invested in large-cap stocks over the years. As of June 2026, the latest data available, their ownership in Nifty 50 stocks fell to a fourteen-and-a-half-year low of 21.1%.
At the same time, from its then all-time high level reached in late September 2024, the Nifty 50 index has fallen a little under 13%. For the massive selling carried out by FIIs that’s not much of a fall.
This has happened because Indian retail investors have been happy to buy what’s being sold by FIIs. The net inflows into equity mutual funds stood at ₹4.2 trillion in 2024-25 and ₹3.5 trillion in 2025-26. From April to September 2026, the net inflows into these funds have stood at ₹1.8 trillion.
So, from April 2024 to September 2026, the net inflows into equity MFs have been ₹9.5 trillion.
Indeed, not all of this money has gone into buying Indian stocks, but a bulk of it has. Also, the money being invested into equity MFs, need not necessarily be going into large-cap MFs, which are mandated to invest a bulk of the money they collect into large-cap stocks. 
But given that the Nifty 50 stocks form close to 54% of the free-float market capitalisation of stocks listed on the NSE, there is no way fund managers can avoid buying these stocks, even when money is being invested in non-large-cap MFs.  
Further, even more retail money has come into the stock market through insurance schemes and government pension and provident funds like National Pension System, the Employees’ Provident Fund and more.
Beyond this, retail investors have also bought stocks directly. There has been an explosion in the number of demat accounts post the pandemic. 
The Selling Math
The FIIs have net sold ₹4.9 trillion worth of stocks from April 2024 till date. The retail inflows have been significantly more than this amount.
The solid retail inflows have not just allowed FIIs to sell, the promoters of Indian companies have also taken this opportunity to take some cash off the table.  
The ownership of private promoters in stocks that make up for the Nifty 50 companies has fallen from around 29.8% in March 2024 to 27.1% in June 2026.
When promoters sell, it tells us that they believe that their businesses are not worth as much as the stock market thinks they are. Hence, it makes sense for them to cash in at a higher price. 
The retail inflows have also allowed many companies to list on the stock market through IPOs at extremely high valuations, allowing promoters and venture capitalists to make a killing. This includes many startups which hope to make some money someday. 
So, that explains how all this selling has been absorbed without the market really crashing. This is the great Indian wealth transfer, from retail investors to the hyper-rich fat cats.
But it doesn’t explain the ease of liquidity and the ease of availability that the finance minister Sitharaman talked about.
